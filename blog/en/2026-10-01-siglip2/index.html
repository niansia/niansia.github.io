SigLIP 2: why is a model trained to describe images so good at catching fakes?

Swapping my backbone from CLIP to SigLIP 2 made the score on distorted images jump, more than any trick I had spent days tuning. After reading the paper I have two guesses, the self-supervised losses added late in training and the missing CLS token that made me use patch averages. A good backbone raises the starting point; good training pushes it further.

SigLIP 2: Multilingual Vision-Language Encoders with Improved Semantic Understanding, Localization, and Dense Features

Michael Tschannen, Alexey Gritsenko, Xiao Wang, Muhammad Ferjad Naeem, et al.

Paper ↗Code ↗

Why this paper

The biggest surprise in my recent experiments came from swapping the backbone from CLIP-L to SigLIP2 So400m. With the same frozen features and the same evaluation, the AUC on distorted images jumped a long way, more than any trick I had spent days tuning. Stranger still, SigLIP2 using only its last layer already beat CLIP with several layers concatenated. I wanted to know what it learns during training that CLIP doesn't.

What the paper does

SigLIP 2 is Google's image-text encoder. The first SigLIP replaced CLIP's softmax contrastive loss with a sigmoid loss; the second version combines several separately developed training methods into one recipe:

  • The original sigmoid image-text loss, used throughout.
  • LocCa-style captioning pretraining, also used throughout: a text decoder learns to write image captions, describe local regions, and locate a region from its description.
  • Self-distillation and masked prediction (from SILC and TIPS), added only for the last 20% of training. Self-distillation has a student that sees only local crops (8 local views) match the representation a teacher (an EMA of the student) computes from the full image; masked prediction replaces half of the student's input patches with mask tokens and asks it to predict the teacher's features at those positions. The paper says the goal is better local semantics in the un-pooled patch features.
  • Data: WebLI's 10 billion images and 12 billion alt-texts in 109 languages, with de-biasing; the two smallest models also get active data curation.

The models come in four sizes, ViT-B (86M), L (303M), So400m (400M) and g (1B). They beat the first version at every size, with especially clear gains on localization and dense prediction. Pooling uses a MAP head (attention pooling) and there is no CLS token. There is also a NaFlex variant that keeps the native aspect ratio and supports several resolutions.

Compared with my experiments

Guess one: the self-supervised losses make the features more robust to distortion. CLIP only learns which caption goes with which image, so once the semantics are right, other details can be ignored. SigLIP 2 additionally asks the model to give a representation consistent with the full image when it sees only part of it, or half of it is masked out. That is about crops and occlusion, not JPEG or noise, but in spirit it resembles the "clean and distorted copies should agree" term I added to my LoRA training. If the guess is right, SigLIP 2 has already done part of the invariance work for me during pretraining, which is why its frozen features are so strong.

Reading the full paper, though, I found that these two losses are only added in the last 20% of training. The advantage could equally come from LocCa, which runs throughout: describing local regions also forces every patch to retain detail. I can't separate the two yet, and the paper never discusses fake-image detection or robustness to distortion, so all of this is my own inference.

Guess two: having no CLS token may actually help. Because the SigLIP family has no CLS token, I take the average of all patch tokens at each layer instead. Averaging is a form of denoising in itself: regions that were compressed, noised or watermarked get averaged out by the rest of the image, while CLIP's CLS token may be pulled off course by a few damaged regions.

After reading the paper, this guess connects neatly with the first one: what SigLIP 2 strengthens late in training is exactly the un-pooled patch features, and a patch average is built from those features. Also, in the NTIRE 2026 report I read earlier the same day, fifth place compared the CLS token, attention pooling and multi-layer concatenation, and found the average over final-layer patches the most stable.

This guess is easy to test: switch CLIP's per-layer features to patch averages too and see whether the score rises. If it does, part of SigLIP2's advantage actually comes from how I extract features, not entirely from the model. It is the experiment I most want to run after reading, and it takes only a few minutes.

Links to the challenge teams. Fifth place in the challenge used SigLIP2-giant (1B parameters, 384 px), sixth place used two SigLIP So400m models plus two CLIP models, and seventh place also has a 384 px SigLIP 2 branch. It seems I'm not the only one who found this family suits the task. I use the smaller So400m at 224 px. Earlier, raising CLIP from 224 to 336 did nothing for me, so for now I won't chase higher resolution and will keep the budget for data and fine-tuning.

My view

The lesson I take from this paper: choosing the right pretrained model matters more than clever additions downstream. Over several days on CLIP I added multi-depth features, distorted copies and LoRA to push the score up; with SigLIP2 the very first step got roughly as far. That is a little discouraging, but also very practical: had I screened backbones first, I would have saved a lot of time. On my next project I will spend an hour or two screening backbones with cheap methods before committing serious compute.

I don't think the earlier work was wasted, though. Training with my own distortions helps SigLIP2 just as much, and combining different backbones still adds more. A good backbone raises the starting point and good training pushes it further; the two add up.

The paper's emphasis on multilinguality and fairness may look unrelated to my task, but it means more diverse training data. What fake detection fears most is content and styles it has never seen, so a model pretrained on more diverse data may simply generalize better.

What I want to try

  1. Per-layer patch averages for CLIP (a few minutes): check whether "no CLS, use the average" is part of SigLIP2's advantage.
  2. LoRA fine-tuning of SigLIP2 (about five hours): on CLIP, LoRA brought a solid gain, and SigLIP2 should have room too.
  3. The aspect-preserving NaFlex variant: my weakest category is crops that change the aspect ratio, which an aspect-preserving SigLIP2 might handle directly. But keeping the aspect ratio didn't help on CLIP, and the paper says NaFlex, for simplicity, skips self-distillation and masked prediction; if guess one is right, it may lose part of that benefit. So I'm keeping expectations low.
  4. Borrow "local versus global" for my LoRA: besides clean versus distorted, add consistency between local crops and the full image, and see whether that helps the aspect-ratio crops.
