RINE: do intermediate layers know “fake” better than the last one?

RINE detects generated images from CLIP's intermediate layers, and my own experiments largely agree. Under real-world corruptions, though, the most useful layers change, and the breadth of augmentation matters more than which layer the features come from.

2026-09-305 min readAI-generated image detectionCLIProbustness

ECCV 2024 · Read closely

Leveraging Representations from Intermediate Encoder-blocks for Synthetic Image Detection

Christos Koutlis, Symeon Papadopoulos

Paper ↗Code ↗

Why this paper

An experiment this week gave me a surprise. With the same frozen CLIP ViT-L/14, replacing "the last layer's CLS" with "CLS tokens from a few depths, concatenated" clearly raised the AUC on corrupted images of the hard validation set. It is the second-largest gain in my whole project, and it costs almost nothing.

Only afterwards did I find that RINE had made this the core of a paper. I wanted to know two things: do they see the same thing I do, and do their conclusions hold in the harder setting of real-world corruptions?

What the paper does

CLIP's image encoder (ViT-L/14) has 24 Transformer blocks. The usual approach (UnivFD, for example) classifies from the last block only, but the last block is trained for semantics, and lower-level information such as generation artefacts may already be lost by then.

RINE takes the CLS token from each block (the best setting uses all 24), passes them through a light network, and adds a Trainable Importance Estimator (TIE) that learns which blocks matter. The loss is binary cross-entropy plus a supervised contrastive term with a tunable weight.

What stood out to me:

  • Little training data: trained only on ProGAN images, with as little as one object class (horse), it generalises to 20 test sets. The 4-class model beats the previous state of the art by 10.6 points in average accuracy (4.5 in AP).
  • Cheap training: one epoch, about 8 minutes.
  • Intermediate blocks matter: many intermediate blocks win the highest learned importance more often than the last one.
  • Bigger is better: ViT-L/14 beats ViT-B/32 in every training setting.

Where my results agree

  1. Intermediate layers help, and it is a cheap, large gain. I see the same trend on several CLIP and DINOv2 backbones: multi-depth features consistently raise corrupted-image AUC by several points. RINE's conclusion holds across backbones and data, which makes me more confident it is not a fluke.
  2. ViT-L/14 is much better than smaller models. Moving from B/32 to L/14 is a clear gain, even with the last layer alone.
  3. Little training is needed. RINE needs one epoch; in my frozen-feature setup the classifier head trains in minutes. "Frozen large model + light head" really is something a laptop can do.

Where my results differ

This is the part I find most interesting.

First, the most important layers change with corruption. I tested CLIP-L/14 layer by layer; roughly:

Layers Clean images Corrupted images
middle layers most accurate drop the most
last few layers a little worse the most stable

The middle layers are accurate but fragile. RINE's TIE learns one fixed set of weights, from essentially clean training images, so what it learns as "important" is probably "important on clean images". That would explain why, in RINE's own robustness test, single perturbations such as cropping or compression barely hurt, while combining several perturbations lowers performance considerably.

Second, they avoid resizing on purpose; my data points the other way. RINE skips resizing in training and testing because it erases generation traces, which is reasonable in their setting. But the NTIRE challenge data re-encodes every image with the same JPEG settings, and resizing is the most common corruption. I found that the whole image resized to 224 is the most robust view, and the trained model relies almost only on it: crops taken at native resolution give nearly identical predictions. Hand-crafted high-frequency noise features are close to chance.

In other words, in the wild the premise of "keep the low-level traces" may not hold at all. What the model ends up using looks more like the mid-to-high-level "something looks off" cues.

Third, breadth of augmentation matters more than architecture. RINE's corruption augmentation is only blur and JPEG. In my experiments, training on dozens of corruptions chained at random, with the same features and the same data, raised corrupted-image AUC by a wide margin, and even JPEG-AI, which I never simulated, improved with it. That gain is larger than the one from multi-depth features.

So in the wild, "how many kinds of damage the model saw in training" matters more than "which layer the features come from", and doing both works best.

What I think

I like how simple RINE is: no change to the backbone, no large-scale fine-tuning, just a different answer to "where to look", and cross-generator generalisation improves a lot. Cheap, reproducible ideas like this are exactly the path my "8 GB laptop GPU" project wants to take.

But its evaluation leans towards generalisation across generators on clean images, not robustness when the same image goes through post-processing. These are different questions: the first asks "can it catch a generator it never saw?", the second "can it still catch the image after compression, resizing or a watermark?". My experiments suggest that in the second case, some of the intermediate layers that help most on clean images become a weakness. Judging only from clean images makes it easy to overrate them.

Public benchmarks also often contain a shortcut: real and fake images differ in size or JPEG settings, so a model may learn those differences instead of generation traces. The NTIRE organisers made all of this uniform, which may also be why low-level cues are almost useless on my data. It makes me a little more cautious about low-level-trace methods that look great on older benchmarks.

What I want to try next

  1. Layer weights that adapt to corruption: instead of one fixed importance vector, first estimate how badly the image is damaged, then decide how much to trust middle versus deep layers. Clean images lean on the middle layers; heavily compressed ones on the deep layers.
  2. Add TIE to my LoRA model and compare it with the current "multi-depth CLS concatenated + MLP".
  3. Compare the two losses that pull features together: their supervised contrastive loss says "same class, close together"; my consistency loss says "the clean and corrupted versions of one image should agree". Maybe the two can be combined.
  4. Add a related-work section to my research notes that states clearly how UnivFD, RINE and my approach differ, so the work holds up better when it goes public.

The paper's numbers were checked against the arXiv HTML version (v2). My experiments are still in progress, so this note gives only the rough trends; the full numbers and setup will follow once they are written up.
