## Why this paper

The problem we have been working on: a long video has too many frames, so it has to be compressed to fit into the model. But what if a later question asks about a detail that was compressed away? How do you get that piece back, and how do you put it back where it was in the video?

Most methods that reduce visual tokens follow the same recipe: score them, keep the high scorers, delete the rest, this paper questions the deleting itself. It works on images, not video, but it asks exactly our question: does something that was dropped get a chance to come back?

## What the paper does

**First, an observation.** How important a visual token is changes with decoder depth. The paper follows one token that lies inside the target box for "man in green shirt": its attention percentile is only 0.11 at layer 3 (where FastV decides) and 0.25 at layer 8 (where PDrop decides), so both methods delete it; by layer 25 it has climbed to 0.97. A token that looks like background in shallow layers is key evidence in deep ones.

**The method is simple.** Reroute keeps the pruning method's scorer (text-to-vision attention) and its stage schedule, and changes only what happens after scoring:

- high-scoring tokens go through the stage's attention and FFN as usual;
- low-scoring tokens are **not deleted**. They skip this stage (along the residual path, their representation unchanged) and are re-ranked against **all** visual tokens at the next decision point, returning if their score is high enough.

Ordinary pruning is the special case where coming back is forbidden. In the equations the two differ only in the next stage's candidate set: the tokens kept last stage, or all of them. Seen another way, this is a training-free Mixture-of-Depths with attention scores as the router. Because each stage computes over the same number of tokens, the theoretical TFLOPs and KV cache stay in the same class as the pruning method it builds on.

## Results

The main task is grounding (the RefCOCO family; a predicted box counts if its IoU exceeds 0.5). Below are LLaVA-1.5-7B (HuggingFace format) averages, relative to the full model with all 576 tokens:

| Avg. tokens kept | FastV | FastV + Reroute | PDrop | PDrop + Reroute |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| 192 | 45.5% | 59.2% | 71.3% | 82.3% |
| 128 | 19.8% | 31.8% | 53.4% | 61.2% |
| 64 | 0.6% | 0.6% | 22.2% | 34.0% |

The main points:

- **The harder the compression, the more it recovers, but only if there is a second look.** FastV decides once, at one layer, and at 64 tokens almost nothing comes back; PDrop decides in several stages, and Reroute lifts it from 22.2% to 34.0%.
- **It holds on other backbones.** On Qwen2.5-VL-7B with 88.9% of tokens removed, PDrop goes from 11.1% to 18.6%; the hybrid Qwen3.5-9B gains the most, with FastV going from 31.1% to 77.5% at two-thirds removed.
- **General QA does not suffer.** Across eight VQA benchmarks including GQA, TextVQA and MME, the average matches or beats the pruning baseline.
- **Compute is nearly the same.** At 64 tokens, PDrop + Reroute cuts TFLOPs by 77% and KV cache by 83%, close to PDrop itself.
- Also worth noting: every experiment ran on **a single RTX 4090**.

## Two things in the appendix I care about most

**First, position indices matter more than I expected.** After pruning, the kept tokens can be renumbered into consecutive positions or keep their original indices. With FastV keeping 192 tokens on LLaVA-1.5, renumbering drops RefCOCO testA from 27.8 to 1.9, almost all grounding ability gone. The tokens are still there, but the model no longer knows where they came from.

For our problem this is a direct warning: getting a token back is only half the job; it also has to go back to the right place. Video adds a time axis, and a token that returns with the wrong time position will be read as a different moment.

**Second, it gets worse on Qwen3-VL.** In the appendix's Qwen3-VL-8B experiment with two-thirds of tokens removed, PDrop scores 69.5% and adding Reroute drops it to 57.2%; only at the extreme 88.9% does it help, from 12.9% to 19.1%. The authors attribute this to Qwen3-VL's DeepStack (extra visual features injected into the first few layers): a hand-set schedule has to fit the architecture. The result appears only in the appendix and the main text does not mention it, but Qwen3-VL is exactly the family we work with, so for us it is the most important table in the paper.

## Taking it to long video: what I keep thinking about

An image has a few hundred tokens; a video is another matter. After reading, I kept wondering what "defer instead of delete" runs into on long video:

1. **The candidate pool itself is the burden.** Reroute's deferred tokens stay in the sequence, just outside the computation. An image's 576 tokens fit easily, but a long video has tens of thousands, and keeping all their representations on the GPU is the very memory problem we want to solve. On video, "defer" may have to mean "put somewhere else": frequently used tokens on the GPU, the rest in main memory, or even recomputed from the raw frames when needed.
2. **Re-locating needs a cheap index.** Every re-ranking in Reroute computes attention over all candidate tokens. If the candidates are not on the GPU, something cheap has to decide first "roughly which stretch of time", then "which tokens in that stretch": locate in time first, then in space.
3. **Do representations go stale if they skip too long?** A deferred token's representation stays at the layer where it was skipped; if it returns several stages later, it carries shallow features into computation with deep text features. The paper does not analyse this, and on video it may be worse: a token can lag behind in both depth and time.
4. **The two kinds of recovery could be chained.** Act2See, which I read in August, goes back to the raw video to fetch frames: expensive, but anything can be found. Reroute picks tokens back up inside the model: cheap, but only what was encoded in the first place. Long video probably needs both: look inside first, and go back to the raw video only when that fails.

After these two papers, I am more and more convinced that compression and recovery should be designed together, not "compress and be done".

## Open questions

- **Real speed is not measured.** The paper reports theoretical TFLOPs and KV cache, and the authors acknowledge that actual latency depends on the gather/scatter implementation. How fast it really runs on a home GPU is what I care about most.
- **How much of the Qwen3.5 jump comes from tokens coming back?** The authors' explanation is that the hybrid's linear-attention layers need the full sequence layout, which Reroute happens to keep. So how much of the gain is "keeping the layout" and how much is "letting tokens return"? A control that keeps the layout but forbids returning would make this much clearer.
- **One number looks odd.** In the VQA table, PDrop keeping 192 tokens scores 46.9 on TextVQA, while keeping fewer, 128, scores 56.2. If that cell is off, the average gap at 192 tokens is inflated.
- **Recovered, but still far behind.** Even with Reroute, Qwen2.5-VL with 88.9% removed keeps only 18.6% of its original score. Some things come back, but full recovery is a long way off, which is where we want to go next.

## What I want to try

1. Apply Reroute as-is to short videos and check whether video-token importance also drifts with depth, tracking one key token like the paper's Figure 2.
2. Build the "keep the layout, forbid returning" control to separate where the gains come from.
3. Compare returning tokens with their original time positions against renumbering them.
4. Measure real latency on my own GPU to see how much the gather/scatter overhead costs.
