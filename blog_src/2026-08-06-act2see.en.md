## Why this paper

When people miss a key moment in a video, they rewind. When a question asks "what if they had done it differently", they picture that scene in their head. Today's video models do neither: they sample frames at a fixed rate up front, and from then on the whole chain of thought is text. If the key frame was not sampled, the model can only guess.

The name says it plainly, Act to See, you have to act before you can see. The model is meant to decide, halfway through its reasoning, whether to go back and find a frame or to generate a hypothetical one. I have long felt this is the right direction, so I read it closely.

## What the paper does

The core idea is two tool calls inside the chain of thought:

- **`<retrieve>`**: the model writes what it wants to find, and TFVTG (a training-free temporal grounding method built on BLIP-2) locates the matching segment; its middle frame is inserted back into the reasoning. Retrieval searches the video at 3 fps, denser than the 1 fps the model started with, so it really can recover "the frame that was not sampled".
- **`<generate>`**: for counterfactual "what if" questions, it first retrieves a related frame, then uses Stable Diffusion 3.5 Large for image-to-image generation to draw the hypothetical scene.

The hard part is that no dataset teaches a model *when* to call these tools. The authors let Gemini 2.5 Pro write chains of thought with tool calls on three datasets that come with human-annotated reasoning (MINERVA, CausalVQA, Social Genome), then filter them in layers:

1. traces with wrong answers are regenerated, at most twice;
2. a trace is kept only if its BGE-M3 similarity to the human-annotated reasoning is above 80%;
3. a format check, and finally a manual check of 100 samples, all of which pass.

That leaves 3,373 samples, 1,608 of which (about 47.7%) contain retrieved or generated frames (1,026 retrievals, 582 generations).

One detail I found clever: the human reasoning is **not** shown to Gemini, only used for filtering. Give Gemini the answer and it barely calls the tools (the ablations show this).

Training itself is plain: LoRA fine-tuning of Qwen3-VL-8B-Thinking with a learning rate of 2.5×10⁻⁶, batch size 1, one epoch, on **eight A100s**. Inference uses the same TFVTG and SD3.5 Large. After training, the model writes `<retrieve>` and `<generate>` into its own reasoning, and that is the "emergent" in the title.

## Results

Against the base model (most base-model numbers were re-run by the authors):

| Benchmark | Base Qwen3-VL-8B-Thinking | Act2See |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Video-MME | 71.8 | 74.2 |
| VideoEspresso | 41.5 | 46.8 |
| EgoNormia | 48.9 | 51.3 |
| VCR-Bench | 38.2 | 47.1 |
| ViTIB | 60.2 | 63.3 |

All five improve, VCR-Bench the most (+8.9). It also leads most open models of the same size. It is still far from Gemini 2.5 Pro (84.3 on Video-MME), and the concurrent Chain-of-Frames scores higher on Video-MME too (75.3).

## Three ablations I care about most

**First, where the data comes from matters more than how much there is.** With the same 1,000 samples, reasoning sourced from VLM-generated datasets (the VideoEspresso and Video-R1 training sets) gives 68.6 on Video-MME, worse than the untuned base model (71.8); human-annotated sources give 73.7.

**Second, giving the answer makes it worse.** Putting the human reasoning into the prompt drops the share of traces with frames from 45.21% to 5.26%, and Video-MME from 73.7 to 72.2. Once you have the answer you stop looking, which is rather human.

**Third, retrieval and generation have clear roles.** In the 1,000-sample runs, text only gives 71.9, retrieval only 72.8, generation only 72.4, and both 73.7. The appendix splits VCR-Bench into counterfactual questions (107, labelled with GPT-5.2) and factual ones, which makes it clearer:

| Tools in the reasoning | Counterfactual | Factual | All |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| None | 35.52 | 38.51 | 38.20 |
| Retrieval only | 43.41 | 45.80 | 45.55 |
| Generation only | 56.01 | 37.70 | 39.60 |
| Retrieval + generation | 56.22 | 46.05 | 47.10 |

Nearly all of the gain on factual questions comes from retrieval. Generation is strong on counterfactual questions (35.52 → 56.01) but slightly *below* using no tools on factual ones (37.70 vs 38.51). The appendix also counts 127 generations on VCR-Bench, 13 of them (10.2%) wrong, most often semantically (7).

## The main reflection: eight A100s

I stopped at the training-details line for a long time. LoRA, batch size 1, one epoch, a few thousand samples: every setting is frugal, and then it says eight A100s. All I have are consumer graphics cards. If a method only runs on a lab cluster, for me it stays a paper I "know about". So I started asking: without that much GPU, with a single home graphics card, how far could this go?

### First: where do the resources actually go?

Taking the pipeline apart, fewer steps need a big GPU than I expected:

- **Making the data**: the reasoning comes from Gemini 2.5 Pro, which costs API fees, not a local GPU.
- **Retrieved and generated frames**: these are fixed when the dataset is built. Training only reads images; TFVTG and SD3.5 do not have to run during training.
- **Fine-tuning**: this is what needs the GPU, and what it needs is mostly **memory**, not compute. A few thousand steps at batch size 1 is not many; the trouble is that every sample is a long video. Even at one or two hundred visual tokens per frame, a hundred-odd frames is over ten thousand tokens, and the 8B model's weights alone take about 16 GB in bf16.

So for an individual the question is not "can I finish the compute" but "can I fit it in memory". The paper does not say how the eight cards were used or how long training took; my guess is mostly data parallelism, trading cards for time.

### What I would change for a single home GPU

1. **A smaller model plus 4-bit quantisation (QLoRA).** Qwen3-VL comes in 2B and 4B sizes. Quantised to 4 bits, the 8B weights shrink to roughly 5 GB; with gradient checkpointing (batch size is already 1, with gradient accumulation if needed), a 24 GB 3090 or 4090 might run the 8B model directly. On an 8 GB laptop like mine, it would have to be 2B or 4B.
2. **Look a little first, zoom in later.** Use few, low-resolution frames at the start, and full resolution only for retrieved key frames — glance at the whole scene, then pick up the binoculars where something looks off. Visual tokens are the biggest memory cost, so this saves the most.
3. **Turn retrieval into a lookup.** Instead of running TFVTG every time, encode every frame once with CLIP or SigLIP and store the vectors. Retrieval then encodes the query text and takes one dot product to find the closest frame. The video is encoded once and after that retrieval costs almost no GPU; the price may be less precise localisation, which needs testing.
4. **Generate only when needed, with a smaller model.** The appendix is clear: generation helps a lot on counterfactual questions and costs a little on factual ones. So call it only when the model judges a question counterfactual; swap in SD3.5 Medium or the distilled, few-step Large Turbo (the paper's own appendix notes these variants target resource-constrained settings); and if memory is still short, load the VLM and the generator onto the GPU in turn instead of keeping both resident.
5. **Less data, better data.** The 1,000-sample runs already reach 73.7 on Video-MME, against 74.2 for all 3,373 samples. The mixtures differ, so this is not a clean comparison, but the direction is clear. Together with the "VLM-generated sources fall below the base model" result, for an individual the time is better spent filtering data than renting GPUs.

### Turned around: active looking is itself a way to save compute

Only while writing this did I notice: the spirit of Act2See is "you don't have to see everything at once, go back when you need to", which is a memory-saving idea in itself. Rather than packing two hundred frames in at once, give the model a few dozen and let it decide where to look again.

The appendix touches on this, but only halfway. On VCR-Bench, halving Act2See's input frames (0.5 fps, 106 frames on average) gives 38.7%, just above the base model at 1 fps (210 frames, 38.2%), while compute drops from 1025 to 877 TFLOPs. So active retrieval makes up for roughly half the frames, but Act2See's 47.1 at 1 fps is gone as well.

My guess is that the model was trained at 1 fps, so it learned "usually see clearly, occasionally fetch a frame", not "usually see little, go looking actively". If it only got a few frames from the start of training, forcing it to rely on retrieval, would it learn to search better? If so, for a home GPU Act2See would not just be a trick that adds points but the way to fit long videos into 8 GB. That is what I most want to test after reading it.

## Open questions

- **"Emergent" is a strong word.** The model was fine-tuned on data that contains tool calls, and it calls tools at inference, which looks more like it picked up Gemini's habits. What would surprise me is a tool use that never appears in the training data.
- **The Appendix F figure caption says "Qwen3-VL-3B baseline", while the text and table say 8B.** Probably a typo, but it made me wonder whether they tried smaller models.
- **Inference cost is reported only as FLOPs.** How long one generation takes, and whether SD3.5 Large and the VLM must sit on the GPU together, matter a lot in deployment and are not discussed in detail.

## What I want to try

1. If their data is released, rerun the 1,000-sample version with Qwen3-VL-2B or 4B and QLoRA on my own GPU, first checking whether the gap between "text only" and "with retrieval" survives at the smaller size.
2. Replace TFVTG with precomputed CLIP/SigLIP frame vectors and compare accuracy and speed.
3. Try a "few initial frames + active retrieval" training setup, to see whether fewer frames can catch up with the 1 fps result.
4. Turn generation on only for counterfactual questions, and measure how much accuracy drops and how much time it saves.

## Postscript (added in October)

After reading this paper, the question of whether a home GPU could do it stayed with me. Later we built our own method for one of the video reasoning benchmarks in the paper, with Act2See as the baseline. Ours runs on Qwen3-VL-4B on a single RTX 5080 in my own computer, and it scores slightly higher than Act2See.

In the paper, Act2See is an 8B model trained on eight A100s; ours is 4B on one consumer card. The margin is small, but it convinced me that the question I wrote down in August does not have to be answered with "lots of GPUs". Details and numbers will follow once they are written up.
