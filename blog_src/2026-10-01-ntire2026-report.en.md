## Why this paper

This is the exam paper for my whole project. For days I had used the leaderboard numbers as targets without reading closely how each team got there. After a recent backbone change my score jumped noticeably, and I started to wonder where the clear gap between the top two teams and everyone else comes from, and whether it is a gap I could close.

## What the report says

**Data.** 108,750 real photos and 185,750 generated images from 42 generators released between 2022 and 2026, open-source and commercial. Prompts for the generated images are rewritten from captions of matching real photos, and resolution, aspect ratio and JPEG quality are aligned with the real set, so detectors have to learn features that don't depend on content.

One design choice I had missed: **the official training set covers only 20 open-source generators**. The strongest open models and all the commercial ones (Nano Banana, Grok Imagine and others) are kept for validation and test, with newer models making up a growing share in each later stage.

**Distortions.** In the robust track every image goes through 1 to 5 consecutive distortions, 36 types across the challenge, from Gaussian blur and white noise to invisible watermarks, neural compression and adversarial watermark removal. Each stage uses a different mix, and the mixes get harder.

**Scale and ranking.** 511 people registered and 20 teams submitted valid final solutions. The public and hidden test sets have about 2,500 images each, and the final rank uses the average robust AUC over the two. The report concludes that most solutions are expert ensembles of several Transformer models and that, although performance has improved a lot, **the problem is not yet solved**, with room left in design, training strategy and data curation.

## The big takeaway: much of the gap is data

I had assumed the winners were strong because their models were big. Reading the report, the top two also had **far bigger and newer training data**: the winner used official APIs to generate images from commercial models such as Seedream, Kling, GPT-Image and Nano Banana Pro, and the runner-up trained on about a million images. I currently use only a small part of the official training data, all of it official, more than an order of magnitude less than the runner-up.

The generator lists in the appendix made me even surer. The public test set includes Nano Banana Pro and Seedream 5 Lite, and the hidden test set includes Nano Banana 2 and GPT Image 1.5, all commercial models the official training set deliberately left out, and the winner's self-generated images come from exactly those families. External data was allowed, so this is entirely within the rules; but it means part of the leaderboard gap measures who has seen more kinds of generators, not only how capable the model is.

That made me rethink my own research question. I want to know how close a small model can get to a large one, but the leaderboard gap mixes three things at once: **model size, data volume and generator coverage, and compute**. Comparing final scores alone cannot tell you where you lose. So I think comparisons should be split:

- Against teams that used no external data, such as Vincentlc in fifth place (the report mentions none). With a 1B-parameter SigLIP2-giant at 384 px, its conditions are the closest to mine.
- Against the top two, with their large external data, stating honestly that the conditions differ.

## Compared with my experiments

**Where they agree:**

1. **Adding distortions during training is key.** Nearly every team has its own distortion pipeline. The runner-up uses four levels: clean, mild (1–3 distortions), moderate (3–6) and heavy (a fixed 6). Fifth place runs every training image through the official distortion function and calls it the main driver of their robust score. That matches my biggest finding: with the same features, just adding distortions I generate myself raises the score on distorted images by a large margin.
2. **Squashing to a square is no worse than keeping the aspect ratio.** Fifth place resizes straight to 384 regardless of aspect ratio ("squish"), arguing that random crops can cut away local forensic cues. I had tried an aspect-preserving version and it was no better. Reaching the same choice independently is reassuring.
3. **Only different models complement each other.** Most solutions are ensembles. Seventh place, Reagvis Labs, puts it clearly: image-text features (CLIP/SigLIP), residual forensic features (SRM) and masked-image-modelling features (EVA-02) fail on different images. I also found that SigLIP2 plus CLIP helps while two CLIP models together barely do. What matters is not the number of models but how differently they see.
4. **Patch averages are steadier than the CLS token.** Fifth place compared the CLS token, attention pooling and multi-layer concatenation, and found the average over all final-layer patch tokens the most stable. That echoes how I extract SigLIP2 features, and it is what I want to test on CLIP next (details in the [SigLIP 2 note](/blog/en/2026-10-01-siglip2/)). They also say multi-layer concatenation didn't help, unlike what I saw on CLIP; that may depend on the backbone or setup and is worth checking.

**What I have to admit:** third place, TeleAI, uses pairwise training: each clean image and its distorted copy go into the same batch, and their predictions (KL) and features (MSE) are pulled together. That is very close to the "clean and distorted features should agree" idea in my LoRA training. My details differ (a stop-gradient cosine consistency, plus my own chain of distortions and neural compression), but the general direction is not mine, and my research notes should say so.

**Where they differ:** seventh place uses 8-view test-time augmentation (flips, multi-scale centre crops, corner crops, blur, JPEG), whereas the flips and multiple crops I tried had no effect at all. My guess is that their ensemble includes a forensic CNN that looks directly at raw pixel residuals, which is sensitive to crop position, while my model relies almost entirely on the whole resized image, so moving the crop changes nothing.

## My view

The most valuable line in the report, to me, is the organisers saying the problem is not yet solved. The top two used enormous resources and still make mistakes on the hardest distortion mixes. For me, rather than trying to "beat" 32 A100s with a laptop, the more meaningful question is: **what is the most efficient approach at each level of resources?**

The report happens to give two numbers to compare: the runner-up's full inference processes about 2.2 images per second on one A100 using 78 GB of memory, while seventh place loads its six models one at a time and peaks below 4 GB. The scores differ by a margin; the memory differs by roughly twenty times. How far can you get with official data only and under a billion parameters, and how much score does each doubling of data buy? That cost-versus-score curve may be worth more as research than any single rank.

I don't want to give up on catching the front of the pack, of course. But after reading this I know the biggest lever next is probably not a bigger model; it is **data**.

## What I want to do next

1. **Use all the official data first**: download more official training shards and measure how the score changes each time the data doubles.
2. **Decide on external data**: the challenge allows it, but I need to think through licensing, and it would muddy the small-versus-large comparison. I lean towards official-data-only as the main result and external data as a supplementary experiment.
3. **Graded distortions**: following the runner-up's four levels, turn my current random chain of 1–5 distortions into a curriculum with light and heavy stages, to see whether it shores up the hardest mixes.
4. **Add a "related approaches" section to my research notes**: say clearly which ideas I share with challenge teams (pairwise training, squish, distortion pipelines) and which are my own findings (the trade-off of multi-depth features under distortion, why test-time augmentation does nothing, why only cross-backbone ensembles complement).
